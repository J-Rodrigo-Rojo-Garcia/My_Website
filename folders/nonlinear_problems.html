Bayesian Nonlinear Inverse Problems

We now focus on finite-dimensional nonlinear problems, that is, problems where the forward model is given by a function \(\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{R}^{m} \to \mathbb{R}^{n}\). To begin, we highlight the structure of the posterior distribution, which is given by: $$ \pi_{\text{post}}(\theta \mid y) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Vert \mathcal{G}(\theta) - y \Vert_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}\right) \pi_{0}(\theta) $$ Unlike the linear case, we no longer have a closed-form solution. Therefore, different techniques must be employed depending on the dimensionality of the parameter and data spaces, as well as the prior information available. In general, there is no single formula or universal approach; however, we can suggest several standard methodologies commonly used to address parameter estimation in this context.


Density Visualization

This approach consists of generating a plot of the posterior density over a specific domain. It is only practical in one- or two-dimensional settings due to visualization limitations. Despite this, it is highly useful for identifying the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate, detecting multimodality, and gaining insight into the overall uncertainty of the posterior distribution. However, it does not offer a quantitative method for computing statistical estimators such as the mean, variance, or higher-order moments.


Sampling Techniques

As mentioned in other sections, generating samples is one of the most straightforward ways to obtain quantitative information about the posterior distribution. Here, we can use standard techniques such as rejection sampling or the various forms of MCMC (see Random-Walk section). This is possible because, in Bayesian statistics, the proportionality constant \(Z(y)\) is not required to define the acceptance probability in the Metropolis-Hastings kernel. The acceptance ratio is given by:

$$ a(\theta,\hat{\theta}) = \min\left(\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Vert \mathcal{G}(\theta)-y \Vert_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}\right)\pi_{0}(\theta)q(\hat{\theta},\theta)}{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Vert \mathcal{G}(\hat{\theta})-y \Vert_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}\right)\pi_{0}(\hat{\theta})q(\theta,\hat{\theta})},1\right), $$

where a strategic choice of \(\pi_{0}\) (such as an exponential family density or uniform distribution) can simplify the expression.


Advantages


1) This technique is straightforward, and there are many established sampling methods available.


2) It is possible to estimate statistical quantities (mean, variance, moments) numerically.


3) It enables visual analysis of the posterior through histograms.


4) If the forward map produces a curve, all evaluations can be reused to plot confidence envelopes of the model.


Disadvantages


1) In high-dimensional parameter spaces, convergence to the target distribution can be extremely slow. It may also be difficult to determine the appropriate burn-in period, making the number of required samples prohibitively large to ensure convergence.


2) Some forward mappings are computationally expensive, even in low-dimensional spaces. This leads to long evaluation times for MCMC methods. Moreover, this issue cannot be addressed by parallel computing techniques due to the strong serial dependence inherent in Markov chains.


3) Some methods may struggle to generate samples from multimodal distributions. Once the chain is near one mode, it may take a long time to transition to another MAP estimate.


4) Even when the distribution is not multimodal, the geometry of the posterior may be irregular (see Rosenbrock example). In such cases, adaptive MCMC methods are recommended.


Even though several disadvantages have been noted, this remains one of the most straightforward and widely used techniques for analyzing posterior distributions.


MAP Estimation

Here, the inversion problem is reduced to estimating the MAP (Maximum a Posteriori) point: $$ \theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\theta} \pi(\theta \mid y), $$ which is equivalent to the following minimization problem: $$ \theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_{\theta} \left( \Vert y - \mathcal{G}(\theta) \Vert_{\Gamma^{-1}}^2 - \log(\pi_{0}(\theta)) \right), \quad \theta \in \mathrm{Supp}(\pi_{0}). $$

This method is particularly useful in high-dimensional parameter spaces, as many optimization libraries in Python and MATLAB can efficiently solve such minimization problems. Nevertheless, it does not provide a way to quantify uncertainty in the model. Furthermore, estimating the MAP can become challenging in the presence of multimodal posterior distributions.


Laplace Approximation

This method consists of constructing a local approximation of a unimodal posterior distribution using a Gaussian distribution. In this context, we can apply the formula used for linear problems (see linear case). To do so, we require a linear approximation of the forward mapping, \(\mathcal{G}_{L}\theta \approx \mathcal{G}(\theta)\). Then, given a prior covariance matrix \(C_{0}\), we can compute the posterior covariance matrix \(C_{L,\text{post}}\) as:

$$ C_{L,\text{post}} = \left(\mathcal{G}_{L}^{*}\Gamma^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{L} + C_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}, $$

and the approximated posterior distribution is given by: $$ \pi(\theta \mid y) \approx \mathcal{N}(\theta_{\text{MAP}}, C_{L,\text{post}}). $$

The approximation works well when the forward mapping is smooth. However, it performs poorly when the forward mapping is highly nonlinear (e.g., regions with steep gradients or almost discontinuities), or more generally, when the posterior distribution deviates significantly from a Gaussian shape.
