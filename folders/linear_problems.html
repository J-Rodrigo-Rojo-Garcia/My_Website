Bayesian Linear Inverse Problems

Here, we focus on linear forward mapping operators \(\mathcal{G}\) (especially in finite-dimensional cases) and Gaussian noise, as follows: $$ y = \mathcal{G}\theta + \eta, \quad \eta\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma). $$

At first glance, the linear structure of the forward mapping may simplify the expression of the posterior distribution. However, this is not true for arbitrary prior distributions. Moreover, constructing a prior distribution in high-dimensional spaces is not straightforward.


One way to leverage the linear structure is to use a non-degenerate Gaussian prior distribution \(\pi_{0}(\theta)\sim\mathcal{N}(m_0,C_0)\). As mentioned in the previous section, the log-posterior density is equivalent to Tikhonov regularization, as follows:

$$\log(\pi(\theta \mid y)) = -\frac{1}{2}\Vert y - \mathcal{G}\theta \Vert_{\Gamma^{-1}}^2 - \frac{1}{2}\Vert \theta - m_{0} \Vert_{C_0^{-1}}^2 + K_{1}. $$

It is clear that if we expand the squared norms \(\Vert \cdot \Vert_{\Gamma^{-1}}\) and \(\Vert \cdot \Vert_{C_{0}^{-1}}\) in terms of their respective inner products and complete the square, we obtain a new quadratic form:

$$\log(\pi(\theta \mid y)) = -\frac{1}{2}\Vert \theta - m(y) \Vert_{C^{-1}}^2 + K_{2}(y).$$

Here, we have a new constant \(K_{2}\) dependent on the data, and two new parameters \(m\) and \(C\). The structure of this expression implies that the posterior distribution is Gaussian, with mean and covariance matrix \(m\) and \(C\), respectively. To maintain consistent notation, we denote the mean and covariance matrix as \(m_{post}\) and \(C_{post}\). Furthermore, these parameters are calculated as follows (see e.g., [1–2]): $$ C_{post} = \left(\mathcal{G}^{*}\Gamma^{-1}\mathcal{G} + C_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}, \quad m_{post} = C_{post}\left(\mathcal{G}^{*}\Gamma^{-1}y + C_{0}^{-1}m_{0}\right). $$

Now that we have a closed-form expression for a known posterior distribution, we do not need to compute statistical estimators via Monte Carlo. If a point estimate is needed for the inverse problem, the mean can be used, and we already know the variance and correlation. However, if we want a visual representation in dimensions higher than three, we can generate samples and analyze histograms. In this case, sampling is relatively straightforward using the standard Gaussian formula. We generate an ensemble \(\lbrace z_{k} \rbrace_{k=1}^{M}\) of i.i.d. samples from \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\), then, using the Cholesky decomposition of the posterior covariance \(LL^{*} = C_{post}\), we estimate posterior samples as follows: $$ \theta_{k} = m_{post} + Lz_{k}. $$

Note that computing the Cholesky decomposition and generating a standard Gaussian ensemble are relatively standard techniques.


Classical Examples

1) Deblurring: This problem involves recovering the original image from a blurred observation. Applications include enhancing medical images, license plate recognition, or aesthetic filters for social media apps.


2) Denoising: This task involves removing noise from a dataset. Originally considered a special case of the deblurring problem, denoising has since been extended to various areas of data preprocessing.


3) X-ray Tomography: The analysis of high-density objects using X-rays is treated as a linear inverse problem.


4) Contaminant Source Location: The spread of a contaminant in a fluid is governed by a diffusion-convection model. Locating the source term is a linear inverse problem.


5) Biomagnetic Source Location: This is a problem similar to (4), but applied in the mining industry to locate ferromagnetic minerals.
